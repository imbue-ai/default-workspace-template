Stopping a file watcher now reliably releases the OS watch it holds.

The watcher creates its watchdog observer on its own background thread, but `PathWatcher.stop()` ran on the caller's thread and read the observer as its very first act. In a single `start()` / `stop()` pair that read almost always landed before the background thread got as far as creating the observer: measured over 50 iterations, `stop()` saw no observer 48 times, and in all 48 the background thread went on to start one -- plus its emitter thread and an OS-level watch -- a fraction of a millisecond later, then exited, leaving nobody to stop them. Watchdog's threads are daemons, so the process still shut down cleanly and nothing ever surfaced the leak; the watches simply accumulated for the life of the process.

`stop()` now joins the watch loop's thread before reading the observer, and observer creation is guarded by a lock that `stop()` also takes and by a check of the stop flag. The two outcomes are now exhaustive: either `stop()` sees the observer the loop thread created and stops it, or the loop thread sees the stop request and never creates one. Repeating the same 50-iteration measurement leaves zero observers running.
